# Physical-quality modules and program blueprints

Use these modules after completing the sport-demand analysis. Select only qualities that serve the current sport outcome, health need, or personal goal.

## Universal rules

- Protect one primary outcome; use minimum effective doses for secondary and maintenance qualities.
- Match frequency to training age, sport-practice load, calendar, and recovery rather than an idealized split.
- Progress one main variable at a time: load, repetitions, sets, distance, duration, pace, power, density, complexity, or competition specificity.
- Keep most general strength work around RIR 2–3 during foundation phases.
- Preserve quality in speed, power, precision, and technical work; stop when output or mechanics deteriorate materially.
- Use planned or readiness-driven lower-load periods.

## Physical-quality modules

### Maximal strength

- Use 2–4 weekly exposures depending on priority and sport load.
- Place 1–2 priority movements early; use submaximal, technically repeatable work.
- Choose squat/knee, hinge/hip, push, pull, carry, trunk, or sport-relevant isometric patterns according to need—not as a compulsory checklist.
- Increase load after target work is completed without grinding and with the required RIR.

### Hypertrophy and tissue capacity

- Start near 6–10 challenging sets per target muscle per week for a novice; adjust from recovery and sport overlap.
- Use stable exercises and moderate repetition ranges for most work.
- Account for sport practice as tissue loading; do not blindly add duplicate pulling, jumping, throwing, or impact volume.
- For mass-sensitive sports, distinguish useful muscle from unnecessary body-mass gain.

### Explosive power and reactive strength

- Perform jumps, throws, Olympic-lift derivatives, ballistic presses/pulls, or appropriate implements while fresh.
- Use low repetitions, full intent, sufficient rest, and a stop rule based on speed, height, distance, contact time, or technique.
- Earn advanced plyometrics through landing skill, strength, tissue tolerance, and progression.

### Acceleration and maximal speed

- Use full recovery and high-quality repetitions.
- Progress exposure distance and total high-speed volume gradually.
- Separate acceleration, maximal velocity, and speed endurance when the sport requires different adaptations.
- Do not prescribe unsupervised maximal sprinting to an unprepared or symptomatic athlete.

### Change of direction and reactive agility

- Build braking and planned change-of-direction mechanics before adding reactive complexity.
- Progress from closed drills to perception and decision demands that resemble the sport.
- Count high-intensity cuts, landings, and contacts across technical practice and physical training.

### Aerobic base and endurance

- Use easy continuous, interval, tempo, threshold, or event-pace methods according to the event and phase.
- Progress recoverable duration before repeated high intensity in a deconditioned person.
- Track pace, power, stroke rate, heart-rate trend, RPE, talk test, or distance only when measurement is consistent.
- Choose sport-specific or low-impact modes when they improve transfer or manage tissue load.

### Anaerobic power and repeatability

- Derive work and recovery intervals from event demands; do not default to random circuits.
- Build prerequisite technique and aerobic recovery before high volumes of repeated maximal work.
- Stop when output, pace, power, or mechanics falls beyond the chosen quality threshold.

### Mobility, stability, balance, and coordination

- Target a restriction that affects technique, comfort, or safety.
- Prefer active control in useful ranges over accumulating passive stretching without purpose.
- Integrate balance and coordination with the actual stance, surface, vision, perturbation, and task demands when safe.

### Body composition and weight management

- Preserve strength, technical practice, protein adequacy, and recovery.
- Use modest, monitored changes unless supervised by an appropriate sports-medicine/nutrition team.
- Never use dehydration or extreme restriction as a routine performance strategy.
- Judge changes by performance, trend weight, waist or composition measures, symptoms, and adherence.

## Personal fitness routes without a sport

| Primary need | Common starting structure |
| --- | --- |
| General health / beginner | 2 full-body strength sessions, 2–3 easy aerobic sessions, daily movement |
| Muscle gain | 2–5 strength days according to experience and availability; stable hypertrophy volume and little unnecessary fatigue |
| Basic strength | 2–4 strength days with repeated primary movement patterns and moderate accessories |
| Conditioning | 2–3 easy aerobic sessions, up to 1 hard session initially, and 1–2 strength-maintenance sessions |
| Speed/power | 2 quality exposures plus supporting strength and recovery |
| Mobility/balance | Short targeted practice embedded around strength, skill, and daily movement |
| Fat loss | Strength preservation, daily activity, recoverable aerobic work, modest energy deficit |

These are starting points. Age, health, experience, schedule, preference, and equipment determine the actual plan.

### Fitness-only decision rules

- For a novice with 2–3 training days, default to full-body training rather than a body-part split.
- For a novice seeking general health or recomposition, combine strength, easy aerobic work, and daily movement; do not turn every session into intervals.
- Use upper/lower or another split when four or more days, training experience, enjoyment, and the goal justify it.
- If adherence has repeatedly failed, reduce days, exercise count, and setup complexity before adding motivation tactics.
- Machines, free weights, body-weight exercises, classes, and outdoor activity are tools; choose by safety, access, confidence, progression, and preference rather than ideology.
- Do not require barbell lifts, running, calorie counting, or wearable targets when suitable alternatives better fit the person.
- For older, deconditioned, higher-body-weight, or low-confidence beginners, use supported exercises, low-impact aerobic modes, shorter bouts, balance work when relevant, and gradual exposure.
- For appearance-focused users, still include health and function guardrails; for health-focused users, do not force bodybuilding volume.

## Weekly organization

### Two training days

- Use full-body or two complementary total-body sessions.
- Combine compatible qualities; keep exercise count low.

### Three training days

- Use full-body rotation, high/low/high, or two primary sessions plus one secondary-quality day.
- For sport athletes, place physical preparation around the most important technical sessions.

### Four training days

- Use upper/lower, quality-based days, or two high and two low days.
- Avoid creating four medium-hard days with no real recovery.

### Five or more days

- Count sport practice as training, not as background activity.
- Use microdoses and consolidate stress; higher frequency does not require high daily volume.
- Protect at least one genuine low-load or rest day when the calendar permits.

## Concurrent-training rules

- When the same session must contain multiple qualities, perform technical skill, speed, or power before fatiguing work unless the goal is explicitly fatigue resistance.
- Place the primary outcome first.
- Separate conflicting hard endurance and lower-body strength/power work when practical.
- Easy aerobic work can often coexist with strength training; hard intervals require more deliberate placement.
- Reduce gym volume during competition congestion before removing high-value sport practice.

## Starting-load and intensity calibration

Do not calculate precise KG, pace, or power from body weight alone.

1. Use technique rehearsal and progressive warm-up sets or efforts.
2. Choose a load/output that meets the target with the prescribed RIR, RPE, speed, pace, or technique standard.
3. Record the exact setup, equipment, resistance, repetitions/duration, and response.
4. Reduce about 5%–10% or one progression step when the minimum target or quality standard is missed.
5. Increase by the smallest practical step after the top target is repeated with reserve and stable technique.
6. Use double progression for many novice strength/hypertrophy movements; use sport-specific output thresholds for speed, power, and endurance.

Label units clearly:

- Barbell or trap bar: total load including the bar
- Dumbbell: load per hand unless stated otherwise
- Machine: stack value for that exact machine
- Assistance: larger assistance may mean lower athlete effort
- Implements: mass and specification
- Running/cycling/rowing/swimming: distance, duration, pace, power, stroke/cadence, grade, resistance, or zone as applicable
- Jumps/throws/sprints: height, distance, time, velocity, contacts, or repetitions
